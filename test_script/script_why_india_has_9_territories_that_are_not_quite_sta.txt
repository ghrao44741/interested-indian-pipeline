And this is Union Territories. While most coverage of Indian governance shows you the 28 states, the Chief Ministers, the assembly elections, the coalition dramas — there's this whole other layer that nobody talks about. Nine territories where the central government runs things directly, your local elected government has roughly the power of a very enthusiastic suggestion box, and sometimes there isn't even a legislature at all.

I found this out the hard way when I was trying to explain Indian federalism to someone and they asked me: "So who runs Delhi?" And I confidently said, "The Chief Minister, obviously." Which is technically true. And also deeply misleading. Because the Delhi government controls schools, local roads, some services — but the police? The land? The bureaucracy? That belongs to the Lieutenant Governor. Who is appointed by the central government. Who can, and occasionally does, just overrule the elected government entirely.

This played out most visibly between 2015 and 2022, when the Aam Aadmi Party won Delhi's assembly with a historic majority and then spent the next seven years in a constitutional tug-of-war over who actually ran the city. Courts got involved. Multiple times. The Supreme Court ruled in Delhi's favour in 2023 — and then Parliament passed a law three weeks later that effectively reversed the ruling. Which is either impressive legislative efficiency or something else entirely, depending on your politics.

But why does this system exist at all?

The short answer: India inherited places that didn't fit neatly into any state. Pondicherry was French until 1954 — a proper colony, not just a trading post — and couldn't simply be absorbed into Tamil Nadu overnight. Goa was Portuguese until 1961. The Andaman Islands sit 1,400 kilometres from the mainland, strategically important and logistically complicated. And Lakshadweep has a population of around 64,000 people. That's smaller than most Indian towns. Making it a full state would mean a Chief Minister, a state legislature, a High Court, a complete administrative apparatus — for 64,000 people. The math doesn't work.

So the Union Territory structure is essentially India's solution to the "this doesn't quite fit" problem. Some UTs have their own legislatures — Delhi, Puducherry, and Jammu and Kashmir since 2019. Some don't. Chandigarh exists purely as a shared capital city between Punjab and Haryana, because when the two states were bifurcated in 1966, nobody could agree on who got to keep the original Punjab capital. So it became nobody's — or rather, everybody's — governed directly from Delhi.

And then there's Jammu and Kashmir, which is its own entirely separate essay. It was a full state with special constitutional status under Article 370 for 72 years. Then in August 2019, in roughly 48 hours of parliamentary action, it was split into two Union Territories — Jammu and Kashmir, and Ladakh — and Article 370 was revoked. Whether that was constitutionally valid is still being argued in court.

The point is: Union Territories aren't an administrative accident. They're a deliberate design choice. And like most design choices in the Indian Constitution, they tell you exactly what the framers were worried about — and what they weren't sure how to solve.
