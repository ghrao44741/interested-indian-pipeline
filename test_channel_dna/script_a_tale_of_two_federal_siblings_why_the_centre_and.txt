And this is Centre-State relations. While most coverage shows you tax disputes, Governor clashes, GST Council drama, and increasingly theatrical Chief Ministers yelling about autonomy on Twitter — there's an uncomfortable truth nobody likes to say out loud: the tension isn't a bug, it's the design. For example, the Constitution of India literally hands one sibling the money and the other sibling the responsibility for spending it, then acts surprised when they fight about it every five years.

Meet India's two federal siblings: the Centre and the States. You'd think sharing one constitution would make them get along, right? Nope. One controls the money, the other does the governing. One writes the rules on national security and railways, the other has to explain to angry voters why the hospitals have no beds. One appoints the Governor, the other has to work under that Governor. One can override state laws on a whim if both legislatures happen to pass bills on the same subject, the other just has to live with it. And every five years, they're legally required to sit down with a committee of economists and argue about who deserves what share of the tax revenue — sort of like if your family had a constitutional requirement to fight about the restaurant bill, forever.

Here's the thing that made me actually laugh when I first read it: states in India are responsible for nearly sixty percent of total government expenditure. Health, education, police, roads, water supply — that's all them. But they only raise about thirty-five to forty percent of total government revenue on their own. The rest has to come from the Centre, which collects the big-ticket taxes and then parcels them out based on recommendations from something called the Finance Commission, which is reconstituted every five years under Article 280, and whose recommendations the government can technically ignore but usually doesn't because ignoring them would cause a constitutional crisis and nobody wants to explain that on the evening news.

So the baseline fact of Indian federalism is this: states spend more than they earn, the Centre earns more than it spends, and the gap between those two numbers is the permanent nerve that gets touched every time a Chief Minister accuses Delhi of financial starvation or the Finance Minister accuses a state of fiscal irresponsibility. Both of them are kind of right, which is the most annoying part.

Now, before you say this is just states whining about not getting enough money — hear me out. This arrangement was not an accident. It was imported, nearly intact, from the Government of India Act of 1935, which the British designed specifically to centralize financial control while delegating administrative headaches. The Indian Constitution, adopted in 1950, kept that same structure. It gave the Union government exclusive control over customs, corporate tax, and income tax — the stuff that actually makes money. It gave the states control over land revenue, agriculture tax, and sales tax on goods — which sounded fine in 1950 when India was agrarian and poor, but started looking like a bad deal the moment the economy industrialized and tax revenue shifted overwhelmingly toward services and corporations, which the Centre collects.

The technical term for this is vertical fiscal imbalance, which is bureaucrat-speak for "one level of government has all the money and the other level has all the work." Every country has some version of this. India's own little twist on it is that we baked the imbalance into the Constitution itself and then added an entire institutional process to argue about it every five years, like a recurring calendar reminder to fight.

Let me explain how the division of power actually works, because this is where it starts getting ridiculous. Article 246 of the Constitution, along with the Seventh Schedule, divides all possible legislative subjects into three lists. The Union List has one hundred subjects that only Parliament can legislate on — defense, foreign affairs, atomic energy, railways, airways, currency, banking. High-level, high-revenue, strategic stuff. The State List has sixty-one subjects that only state legislatures can legislate on — police, public health, agriculture, local government, land, water. Ground-level, voter-facing, expensive-to-run stuff. And then there's the Concurrent List, which has fifty-two subjects that both the Centre and the states can legislate on — criminal law, marriage and divorce, contracts, economic and social planning, education, forests.

Here's the kicker: if the Centre and a state both pass laws on a Concurrent List subject and those laws conflict, the central law wins. Always. Article 254 just says it outright. So the Concurrent List isn't really concurrent in the sense of "we share this equally." It's more like "states can legislate here unless the Centre decides it wants to, in which case the states lose."

Think of it like an IPL auction, except one team has an automatic right-to-match card on every single player they want, and the other teams are just there to make it look competitive.

Now, the Finance Commission is where the money fight becomes an official constitutional event. Every five years, the President appoints a bunch of economists and former bureaucrats to figure out how the central tax revenue — the divisible pool, they call it — should be split between the Centre and the states, and then how that state share should be divided among the thirty or so states and union territories. This is called vertical devolution, which is the Centre-to-states transfer, and horizontal devolution, which is the inter-se share — bureaucrat for "your slice of the pie relative to the other states."

The Fifteenth Finance Commission, which made recommendations for 2021 to 2026, recommended that forty-one percent of the Centre's gross tax revenue go to the states. Which sounds generous until you remember that states are responsible for sixty percent of the spending. The gap between forty-one percent of revenue and sixty percent of expenditure is what states have to cover by borrowing, charging user fees, auctioning land, or begging for central schemes with twenty different compliance strings attached.

And here's the fun part: the states that contribute the most tax revenue to the Centre are often not the states that get the most back. Tamil Nadu, for example, contributes about four percent of India's total tax revenue to the central pool. It gets back about three-point-something percent in devolution. Maharashtra contributes fifteen percent of the country's tax revenue. It gets back about six percent. Bihar, on the other hand, contributes about half a percent and gets back about ten percent, because the Finance Commission's formula weighs population, poverty, and fiscal need heavily, not just tax contribution.

Is this fair? Depends who you ask. If you're Bihar, it's equitable redistribution — richer states subsidizing development in poorer states. If you're Tamil Nadu, it's a punishment for being fiscally responsible and economically productive. Both of those arguments are true at the same time. Which is why the Finance Commission is less of a technical body and more of a constitutional ritual for everyone to perform their grievances.

Hearing all this makes it sound like the Centre and the States are locked in a simple tug-of-war over money. But it's not that clean. The money is just the loud part. The quieter, weirder tension is in how governance actually works day to day, and that's where the Governors come in.

Governors are appointed by the President — which functionally means they're appointed by the Union Cabinet, which means they're appointed by whoever runs the central government at the time. But they head the state. They live in the state capital. They're supposed to be above politics. They have the constitutional power to summon or dismiss the state legislature, to reserve state bills for Presidential assent instead of signing them, and to send reports to the President recommending President's Rule if they believe the state government can't function according to the Constitution. Article 356, if you're keeping track.

In theory, the Governor is a neutral constitutional figurehead. In practice, Governors appointed by Party A have a strange habit of clashing with state governments run by Party B. Bills get stalled. Cabinet advice gets ignored. The Governor's official residence becomes the place where defecting legislators hide out during floor tests. This has been happening since the 1950s. It's not new. It's not partisan in the sense that only one party does it — every party in power at the Centre has used friendly Governors to make life difficult for opposition-ruled states. It's partisan in the sense that everyone does it and everyone pretends they don't.

The Sarkaria Commission, which reported in 1988, spent two hundred pages politely suggesting that Governors should stop acting like the Centre's political enforcers in the states. The Punchhi Commission, which reported in 2010, said basically the same thing in even more detail. Neither report changed anything structurally because changing it would require a constitutional amendment, and no government wants to give up a tool they know they'll use the next time they're in opposition at the state level.

So what you end up with is a system where the person who represents the state's constitutional authority is appointed by the Centre, lives in the state, and has the legal power to paralyze the state government if they feel like it. It's like having a referee who's paid by one of the teams. Technically they're supposed to be neutral. Occasionally they are. But the structural conflict of interest is just sitting there, and everyone knows it.

Now let's talk about GST, because this is where the old Centre-State tension got a software update. The Goods and Services Tax, implemented in 2017, replaced a chaotic mess of central excise duties, state VAT, service tax, and about fifteen other overlapping indirect taxes with one unified tax. In theory, this was great for businesses — one tax rate, one filing system, no more checkposts at state borders where trucks got held up for two days while officials argued about whose jurisdiction a shipment of biscuits fell under.

But here's the trade-off: states gave up their independent power to set sales tax rates on goods sold within their borders. That power now sits with the GST Council, created under Article 279A, which is a joint body where the Centre holds one vote and the states collectively hold two votes. Sounds democratic, right? Not quite. The GST Council requires a three-fourths majority to pass any resolution. The Centre alone controls one-third of the total vote weight, which gives it an effective veto over any decision the states might want to push through together.

So the states traded fiscal autonomy for the promise of a smoother tax system and a guarantee of compensation if their GST revenue fell short of a projected fourteen percent annual growth rate. That compensation guarantee was supposed to last five years, from 2017 to 2022. When COVID hit in 2020 and GST collections collapsed, states expected the Centre to make up the shortfall. The Centre said sorry, we don't have the money either, you'll have to borrow it and we'll help you figure out the terms. Several states said that's not what the GST agreement promised. The Centre said well, the law says compensation, it doesn't say we have to pay it out of our own budget.

That fight is still simmering. The compensation guarantee officially ended in 2022. States are now on their own. If GST collections grow, great. If they stagnate, states have no fallback tax they can independently control anymore, because they gave up VAT and sales tax to join GST in the first place. Which is why some state finance ministers now say, in private, that GST was the single biggest centralization of fiscal power since independence. In public, they say it's a landmark reform. Both are true.

I spent two days reading about this and I'm still not entirely sure I understand it. Here's what I do know: the story of Centre-State relations isn't really the states-vs-union fight you think it is. It's a story about a constitutional design that deliberately centralized money and power at the top while decentralizing responsibility and blame to the states, and then built a bunch of formal institutions — the Finance Commission, the GST Council, the Governor's office — to manage the tension without ever actually resolving it.

Every country has some version of this. Federal systems are inherently messy because you're trying to govern a huge, diverse population with one national framework and thirty-something regional frameworks at the same time, and those frameworks are never going to align perfectly. India's version is messier than most because we took the British colonial playbook for centralized extraction, kept the shell, and filled it with democratic elections and political parties that campaign on state pride and regional identity, and then we act confused when Chief Ministers get mad about not controlling their own revenue.

The states that pay in the most don't get the most back. The states that get the most back are often the states with the least capacity to spend it well, which is why fiscal equalization doesn't always translate to equitable development, which is why the average person in Bihar has spent decades doing the math on whether staying home or leaving is the worse bet. Spoiler: both are. The Governor you didn't vote for has more constitutional power over your state legislature than you do. The GST Council can overrule your state's tax policy, and your state gave up the right to object when it signed on. And every five years, a committee of economists you've never heard of will sit in Delhi and decide how much money your state gets to run schools and hospitals, and your Chief Minister will call it either a betrayal or a victory depending on which party they belong to, and none of it will change the structural fact that your state is constitutionally required to spend more than it's allowed to raise.

That's Indian federalism. Not a partnership. Not a rivalry. A permanent negotiation where both sides are holding cards the other side needs, and the rules of the game were written seventy-five years ago by people who thought centralization was safer than trust.
