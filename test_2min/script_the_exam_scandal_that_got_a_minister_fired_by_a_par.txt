In July 2026, a Cabinet minister who had survived every single reshuffle for twelve years resigned. The party that took him down is called, and I promise I am not making this up, the Cockroach Janta Party.

Here's how you get there. In May, India's biggest medical entrance exam, NEET-UG, got cancelled nationwide because a tutor cross-checked a leaked PDF against his students' actual paper and found the questions matched almost word for word. Twenty-two lakh candidates — roughly the population of a mid-sized country — had to sit the same exam twice because somebody couldn't keep a file secret. If you've ever forwarded a "leaked" exam paper in a WhatsApp group as a joke, this is the ministry-level version of that.

Then a Supreme Court judge, in an unrelated hearing, made a remark that got read online as comparing jobless young people to cockroaches. The court said it was taken out of context. Nobody online particularly cared — by the next morning, a student had already announced the "Cockroach Janta Party" as satire. So now a minister who had outlasted a decade of coalition politics was being chased by a party named after an insect. That's where we were.

The government's line, for most of that time, was fix the machine, not the guy who broke it. Then a planned march toward Parliament turned into tear gas and baton charges. I know exactly what you're thinking — either the students went too far, or the police did. Both camps have footage. Both camps have hashtags. What nobody disputes is what happened next: two months of "I'm staying," and then, in about forty-eight hours, he wasn't.

And then the government did the most government thing possible: it announced a brand-new committee to fix the exam system, chaired by the man who built Aadhaar. Which would land better if there weren't already a committee for exactly this, from the 2024 leak, still working through its own hundred-plus recommendations. In Indian bureaucracy, the fix for an unfinished report is always a newer report.
