Here is how a government loses a fight to an insect. India's biggest medical entrance exam is Neet U.G. You pass it, you become a doctor. You miss it, you wait a full year.

In 2026, that exam was cancelled nationwide. The re-exam was pushed to June 21st. Investigators found leaked question banks selling for five lakh rupees. Somewhere between sixty and seventy percent of the real paper matched. The acceptance rate that year was under six percent. Twenty-two point seven lakh students had already sat it in May.

The agency that runs the exam was built in 2017 to stop exactly this. That was the actual sales pitch, word for word. Nine years later, the specialist agency built to protect exam integrity is itself the news story. There had already been a committee, after the last leak, with one hundred and one recommendations. Most of them were reportedly done.

Then a judge, tired of petitions, compared unemployed activists to cockroaches. He meant it as a dismissal. It landed as a gift. Within seventy-two hours a student had turned that one sentence into a satirical page called the Cockroach Janta Party. It reached thirty lakh followers. Not a real party. No candidates, no manifesto, no funding.

Protesters carried cockroach signs to Jantar Mantar. A well-known activist joined with a hunger strike that ran twenty-six days. The government called the whole thing anti-national. On the twentieth of July, police used baton charges. The footage that went viral showed college students holding placards, getting hit with batons.

On the twenty-fifth of July, the education minister resigned. A meme account had outlasted a sitting cabinet minister. The government's response was a brand-new expert committee. Neet U.G. is reportedly moving to computer-based testing next year. Biometric checks, tighter paper security, more surveillance, all promised again.

The real question is not whether India can write a reform report. It has written two. The question is whether next year's students get a fair exam.
