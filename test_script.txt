TITLE: Why India Has 9 Territories That Are Not Quite States

And this is Union Territories. While most coverage of Indian governance shows you the 28 states, the Chief Ministers, the assembly elections, the coalition dramas — there's this whole other layer that nobody talks about. Nine territories where the central government runs things directly, your local elected government has roughly the power of a very enthusiastic suggestion box, and sometimes there isn't even a legislature at all.

I found this out the hard way when I was trying to explain Indian federalism to someone and they asked me: "So who runs Delhi?" And I confidently said, "The Chief Minister, obviously." Which is technically true. And also deeply misleading. Because the Delhi government controls schools, local roads, some services — but the police? The land? The bureaucracy? That belongs to the Lieutenant Governor. Who is appointed by the central government. Who can, and occasionally does, just overrule the elected government entirely. It is, bureaucratically speaking, a very expensive group project where one person can veto everything but refuses to be on the call.

This played out most visibly between 2015 and 2022, when the Aam Aadmi Party won Delhi's assembly with a historic majority and then spent the next seven years in a constitutional tug-of-war over who actually ran the city. Courts got involved. Multiple times. The Supreme Court ruled in Delhi's favour in 2023 — and then Parliament passed a law three weeks later that effectively reversed the ruling. Which is either impressive legislative efficiency or something else entirely, depending on your politics.

But why does this system even exist? I know what you're thinking — this sounds like a temporary arrangement someone came up with in 1950 and forgot to fix. And you're... partly right.

India inherited places that simply didn't fit into any state. Pondicherry was a French colony until 1954. Not a trading post — an actual, administered French territory, with French courts and French law, sitting inside Tamil Nadu like a croissant in a thali. It couldn't just be absorbed overnight. Goa was Portuguese until 1961. The Andaman Islands sit 1,400 kilometres from the mainland. And Lakshadweep has a total population of around 64,000 people. That is roughly half the crowd at an IPL match. Making it a full state would mean a Chief Minister, a state legislature, a High Court, a complete administrative apparatus — for 64,000 people. The cost of governance would exceed the budget of the government. That's like building a five-star hotel and filling it with three guests.

So the Union Territory structure is India's solution to the "this doesn't quite fit" problem. Some UTs have their own elected legislatures — Delhi, Puducherry, and Jammu and Kashmir since 2019. Some don't. Chandigarh exists purely as a shared capital between Punjab and Haryana, because when the two states were bifurcated in 1966, nobody could agree on who got to keep the original Punjab capital. So it became nobody's — or rather, everybody's — governed directly from Delhi. It is co-parenting, but for a city.

And then there's Jammu and Kashmir, which deserves its own video but gets one paragraph here. It was a full state with special constitutional protection under Article 370 for 72 years. Then in August 2019, in roughly 48 hours of parliamentary action, it was reorganised into two Union Territories — Jammu and Kashmir, and Ladakh. Whether that reorganisation was constitutionally valid is still being argued in the Supreme Court.

The point is: Union Territories aren't an accident. They're a deliberate design choice. And like most design choices in the Indian Constitution, they tell you exactly what the framers were worried about — and what they quietly decided to leave for someone else to sort out later. So the next time someone asks you who runs Delhi, the honest answer is: the Chief Minister runs the schools, the Lieutenant Governor runs the police, and the Supreme Court runs the group chat where they argue about everything else.
